# Change Network Methodology

The portable method this skill bakes in. Adapt terminology to the client ("champions," "change agents," "ambassadors," "advocates" — pick one and stay consistent), but keep the mechanics.

## 1. Why a change network

A program touching hundreds of roles across multiple BUs and sites cannot be reached by the OCM team alone — not with the right message, at the right time, in the right local context. The network embeds trusted voices inside the organization who:

- **Translate** — program-level information into team-level relevance.
- **Surface** — concerns, questions, and resistance signals from the front line back to the OCM team.
- **Advocate** — for the change within their peer group; visible early adopters.
- **Support** — colleagues through training, testing (UAT), go-live, and hypercare.

Equally important is what champions are **not**: not project managers (they own no deliverables), not technical SMEs (they don't configure or design), and not spokespeople (leadership messages come through formal channels; champions reinforce and contextualize). State this explicitly in onboarding — role confusion is a top failure mode.

## 2. Tiered model

A two- or three-tier structure, sized to the program:

| Tier | Who | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Sponsor** | Senior executive(s) | Visible authorization: launches the network, recognizes contribution, unblocks nomination stalls. Not a working member. |
| **Change Lead** (per BU or division) | Manager-level, nominated by BU leadership | Coordinates the champions in their BU; first escalation point; attends the lead-level cadence. Only needed when a BU has ~6+ champions. |
| **Champion / Change Agent** (per team or site) | Frontline / individual contributor | The embedded voice: cascades messages, collects feedback, supports adoption locally. |

Small programs (one BU, <200 impacted) can collapse to sponsor + champions. The network is a community, not a reporting line — say so.

## 3. Sizing ratios

- **Baseline: ~1 champion per 20–50 impacted employees.** Use the low end (1:20–25) for frontline/operational populations with shift work and high change severity; the high end (1:40–50) for co-located office populations with lighter impacts.
- Every impacted **team and site** needs at least one champion, even if the ratio says otherwise — a site with 8 impacted people still gets a champion. Local presence beats arithmetic.
- Corporate/cross-cutting functions (finance, loss prevention, procurement) often need only 1 each; frontline operations need many (e.g., 10–12 per large operational division per site).
- Champion time commitment is roughly **10–15% of working time**, varying by phase:

| Phase | Time | Primary activities |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation / Design | ~5–10% | Briefings, initial team conversations |
| Build & Test | ~10–15% | Briefings, UAT support, feedback collection |
| Training & Go-Live | ~15–20% | Training reinforcement, floor support, daily check-ins |
| Hypercare | ~10–15% | Issue surfacing, adoption support, peer coaching |

For lighter-touch networks (small programs, advisory-style champions) 1–4 hours/month is a workable floor — but say which model you're running; don't let expectations drift.

## 4. Selection criteria — influence, not seniority

A good champion:

- **Works in the affected area and is personally impacted** — credibility comes from living the change.
- **Is a trusted, credible peer** — influence comes from respect, not title. The informal go-to person beats the most senior person.
- **Has capacity** — can commit the stated time, with their **manager informed and supportive** (get this in writing via the nomination).
- **Is willing** — voluntold champions underperform; confirm willingness before onboarding.
- **Engages constructively** — skeptics who ask hard questions are fine (often excellent); active detractors are not.

Avoid: known detractors of the program; anyone scheduled to leave, transfer, or be on extended leave during training/go-live; people already overloaded with program roles (SME + UAT + champion in one person burns out).

**Nomination workflow:** OCM defines criteria and seat map (which groups need a champion) → leadership nominates by a deadline (use the nomination_email template; pre-fill everything known so leaders only fill gaps) → OCM screens against criteria and confirms willingness + manager support → onboarding session → seat status moves Open → Nominated → Confirmed → Onboarded. Track open seats visibly — "38 of 55 groups still have no champion" is the sentence that gets sponsor action.

## 5. Operating rhythm (cadence)

The default rhythm; tune frequency to program tempo:

| Touchpoint | Frequency | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Change Network Briefing | Monthly (30 min) | Program updates, key messages, talking points, ONE clear ask, round robin, Q&A. Led by OCM. |
| Office Hours | Bi-weekly | Open drop-in for questions, feedback, challenges. |
| Feedback pulse | After each briefing | Quick survey: what is your team saying/feeling? |
| Resource site | Always on | Latest talking points, FAQs, onboarding guide, briefing packs. |
| Chat channel | Always on | Urgent questions, peer-to-peer support. |
| Sponsor touchpoint | Quarterly / at phase gates | Recognition, network health review, reset of expectations. |

**Briefing design rules** (proven pattern): keep it to 30 minutes; every briefing carries **one clear ask** the champion can act on that week; structure the cascade as verbs (e.g., DRIVE the action, RELAY why it matters, REINFORCE the framing, SURFACE blockers); end with a round robin ("what are you hearing?" — raw signal beats polished synthesis; a quiet BU is itself data); parking-lot rule with a committed follow-up SLA (e.g., 5 business days); send the talking-points doc immediately after so champions cascade in their own words but on message.

**The two-way contract.** OCM gives champions: briefing packs, pre-packaged FAQs (they never answer unarmed), escalation support, recognition. Champions give OCM: post-briefing feedback, early resistance signals, questions/gaps, success stories.

## 6. Recognition & sustainment

- **Sponsor-visible recognition** — named acknowledgment in leadership communications; champions are introduced to the broader org at comms ramp-up, not kept invisible.
- **Professional development framing** — participation documented as development experience; additive to the role, not a performance-review risk (say this explicitly in onboarding FAQs).
- **Milestone recognition** — celebrate at phase gates and go-live; small tokens matter less than public, specific credit.
- **Membership review at phase gates** — refresh the roster; replace departures promptly via leadership, don't let seats silently go dark.
- **Post-go-live**: the network doesn't dissolve at go-live — it carries hypercare (issue surfacing, peer coaching) and can transition into a continuous-improvement or super-user community.

## 7. Common failure modes

1. **Seniority-based nomination** — leaders nominate the most senior or most available person, not the most trusted. Counter: put the criteria in the nomination ask and screen.
2. **No manager buy-in** — champion's day job wins every conflict. Counter: manager informed and supportive as an explicit nomination condition; escalate conflicts to OCM immediately.
3. **One-way channel** — network becomes a comms distribution list; feedback never flows back. Counter: structured feedback pulse after every briefing; round robin in every meeting.
4. **No ask** — briefings become status theater; champions disengage. Counter: one concrete, week-sized ask per briefing.
5. **Coverage gaps ignored** — open seats linger for months. Counter: visible coverage tracking (the dashboard's job) and sponsor escalation with the gap number.
6. **Overload** — same people are champion + SME + UAT tester. Counter: check for role stacking at screening.
7. **Launch-and-abandon** — energy at kickoff, silence by month three. Counter: the cadence is the product; protect it, and recognize participation continuously.
8. **Role confusion** — champions asked to deliver training or defend unpopular decisions. Counter: the "what you are / what you are not" framing in onboarding; escalation path for questions beyond their scope.

## 8. What feeds this skill / what it feeds

- **In:** SHA stakeholder groups (seat map should cover every impacted group), CIA severity by BU (weights the sizing ratio), org/headcount data from leadership (via the nomination workbook — never invent headcounts).
- **Out:** the roster feeds training rollout (champions as training reinforcers/super-user candidates), comms planning (champion cascade as a vehicle), and readiness/adoption measurement (champions as pulse collectors).
